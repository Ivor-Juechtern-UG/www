## The question

The title borrows its shape from "What would Jesus do?", the question Christians ask themselves before acting. It goes back to Charles Sheldon's novel *In His Steps* (1896) and came back in the 1990s as WWJD on wristbands. Asked of Eco, it is not about faith but about method: how would the most widely read man of our time read now?

In one afternoon we went from Rav Kook to Derrida, from Plato's *Phaedrus* to Lamport, from Ricœur to Barthes, and found a Parisian physician writing on the Hebrew letters and artificial intelligence. A generation ago that walk needed a person like Umberto Eco: someone who had spent a life reading, and who carried the map in his head.

Now anyone with a model can walk it at the speed of thought. A pseudo-Eco. What is happening? What is the new value? And would Eco, born today, still be Eco at all?

## The man and his library

Umberto Eco (1932–2016): semiotician, medievalist, novelist. He owned some 50,000 books. His novel *The Name of the Rose* (1980) is built around a library, a labyrinth after Borges, where knowledge is guarded, hidden and finally burned.

His method was slow and physical. In *How to Write a Thesis* (1977) he teaches students to work with index cards: one card per book, per quote, per idea, cross-referenced by hand. The cards were not storage. They were digestion: writing each one forced a decision about what mattered.

Nassim Taleb, in *The Black Swan* (2007), used Eco's library to coin the *anti-library*: the unread books are worth more than the read ones, because they remind their owner of everything he does not know. The library was a monument to humility as much as to knowledge.

## What is happening

The cost of finding has collapsed. The walk from Kook to Barthes used to take years of reading; it now takes minutes of asking. Retrieval, cross-reference and translation, the visible part of erudition, have become cheap.

What has not become cheap is the invisible part: knowing which of a thousand results matters, seeing that Toledano's question is the one worth five underlines, and putting one's name under the judgment.

## Eco saw it coming

Eco was not naive about the network. He compared the web to Borges' *Funes the Memorious*, the man who remembers everything and therefore cannot think, because thinking means forgetting differences, abstracting, choosing. A memory without a filter is not knowledge; it is noise.

In 2015 he said that social media had given the right to speak to "legions of idiots" who used to talk only at the bar. Crude, but the point beneath it is about filtering: the old gatekeepers were gone, and nothing had replaced them.

And in *The Infinity of Lists* (2009) he showed that culture has always answered infinity with lists: catalogues, inventories, encyclopaedias. Every list is a choice of what goes in.

## What would he do today?

He would become the filter. Not the one who finds, but the one who chooses. He would use the machine as his index cards and his assistant, and spend his own life on what the machine cannot do: deciding what matters, connecting what nobody asked to connect, and laughing at it. Eco's erudition was always playful; the play was the point.

He would probably also write the novel of it. A labyrinth, again, but this time without walls: a library where every book can be summoned and none is read.

## Would he be Eco at all?

This is the hard part. Eco's mind was formed by friction: by slow reading, by the card, by the book that took a month. The effort was not a cost on the way to the mind; it was how the mind was built.

The pseudo-Eco gets the reach without the digestion. He can walk the map but has not drawn it. The risk is a generation of Funes in reverse: everything retrievable, nothing remembered.

And yet the walk this afternoon was not done by the machine alone. The machine found; a person chose, rejected, asked again, and said: this one, underline it. That is the Eco part, and it remains human.

## Something honourable

In David Lean's *Lawrence of Arabia* (1962), the men loot a Turkish train after the raid and carry off whatever they can grab. Auda abu Tayi comes away from the first train with a battered umbrella. He will not go home with that: only after the next raid, when he takes horses, does he have something honourable to bring back, and then he leaves.

That is the search of meaning in the age of AI. The loot is endless now, every answer in seconds. What we bring home has to be the one thing that carries honour.

## The new value

- **Judgment over retrieval.** Finding is cheap; choosing is not.
- **The anti-library, inverted.** The humility is no longer in the unread books on the shelf, but in knowing that a fluent answer is not yet understanding.
- **Digestion by writing.** The index card returns as the note: writing it down is still how a thought becomes one's own (Lamport: if you're thinking without writing, you only think you're thinking).
- **Accountability.** A name under the selection. The machine does not sign.

Eco today would not be a man with 50,000 books. He would be a man with one good question and the courage to throw away 49,999 answers.
