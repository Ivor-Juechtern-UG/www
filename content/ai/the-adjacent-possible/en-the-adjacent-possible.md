Good ideas often come before the technology that can carry them, and then wait decades. This article asks how to tell an idea that is waiting from one whose time has come.

## The idea

An idea becomes real only when the steps next to it exist. Two thinkers gave this a name.

The biologist Gunther Stent called such ideas premature. In "Prematurity and Uniqueness in Scientific Discovery" (*Scientific American*, December 1972) he argued that a discovery is set aside when it cannot yet be logically connected to what is known. It is accepted once advances on nearby problems make the connection.

The biologist Stuart Kauffman spoke of the adjacent possible: from any state, only the next steps are reachable, not the far ones. Steven Johnson carried the term into innovation in *Where Good Ideas Come From* (2010). Each step opens a door to a new room, and that room has more doors.

Stent explains why an idea waits. Kauffman explains when its wait ends: when the room next to it has been built.

## Examples

The gap between the thought and its use runs from decades to more than a century. In each case the idea was sound; one neighbouring step was missing.

Mutation testing is the case closest to software today: for forty years it was too expensive to write and run. Large language models write the tests, and idle cores run them overnight.

## What brings an idea into reach

The examples share three things that end the wait.

- **The missing neighbour arrives.** Often it comes from another field: valves from radio for the computer, graphics chips from games for neural networks, nanoparticles from drug delivery for mRNA.
- **The cost falls.** Mutation testing was always possible in principle, but no one could afford to write and run the tests. When the cost of a step drops by orders of magnitude, ideas that waited behind it become practical at once.
- **Someone carries it through the wait.** Karikó worked on mRNA for decades with little funding. Ideas survive their prematurity because a few people keep them alive until the neighbour arrives.

An idea whose neighbour exists and whose cost has fallen is no longer premature. It is in the adjacent possible.

## Close to breakthrough

For funded research, the question is not whether an idea is good but whether its neighbours exist. A project close to breakthrough is one that waits on no missing step.

Three questions follow from the examples:

1. **Which step is it waiting for?** If it names a tool, a material or a dataset that does not exist yet, it is premature, however sound.
2. **Has the cost of that step fallen?** Sequencing, computing and now large language models have made whole classes of experiments cheap. Ideas that waited behind them may be ready now.
3. **Who carries it?** A team that has kept the idea alive through the wait knows exactly which step it needs.
